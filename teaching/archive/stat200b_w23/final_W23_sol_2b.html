Final W23 – Solution to 2b

The problem requires the implicit assumption that \(\|u\|\) remains fixed as \(n \to \infty\), although \(u\) has to change (since its dimension is changing). This is not strictly necessary, although it is easier in this case to think about the consistency question, since we are estimating a fixed parameter.

  1. Reparametrizing MLE: The likelihood is proportional to \[\begin{align*} \ell(u) &\propto_u \frac12 e^{-\frac12\|y - u\|^2} + \frac12 e^{-\frac12 \|y+u\|^2} \\ &\propto_u e^{-\frac12 \|u\|^2} \cosh(\langle y,u\rangle) \end{align*}\] Let us parameterize in terms of \(\alpha = \|u\| \in [0,\infty)\) and \(\omega = u / \|u\| \in \mathbb R^n\) (for \(\alpha = 0\), we can define \(\omega\) arbitrarily). We have \(u = \alpha \omega\). Then, \[\begin{align*} \ell(\alpha, \omega) \propto e^{-\frac12 \alpha^2} \cosh(\alpha \langle y,\omega\rangle). \end{align*}\] To obtain the MLE of \((\alpha,\omega)\), we need to maximize the above function over both arguments. We first maximize over \(\omega\), and then maximize over \(\alpha\). When we maximize over \(\omega\) the solution potentially depends on \(\alpha\), but in this particular case, the maximizer over \(\omega\) is independent of \(\alpha\), hence it actually gives us the MLE of \(\omega\) directly.

  2. MLE of \(\omega\): Maximizing over \(\omega\)—by Cauchy-Schwarz and since \(\cosh\) is increasing on \([0, \infty)\)—we obtain \(\widehat{\omega} = \pm y / \|y\|\). Note that the solution does not depend on \(\alpha\), hence \(\widehat \omega\) is the MLE of \(\omega\). Both \(\pm\) give the same value of the likelihood, hence both can be used as the MLE, that is, the MLE of \(\omega\) is not unique, going back to nonidentifiability.

  3. MLE of \(\alpha\): Plugging in \(\widehat\omega\) into the \(\ell(\alpha, \omega)\), we have (note that \(\cosh(t) = \cosh(-t)\) for all \(t\)) \[\begin{align*} \ell(\alpha, \widehat\omega) \propto_\alpha e^{-\frac12 \alpha^2} \cosh(\alpha\|y\|). \end{align*}\] To obtain the MLE of \(\alpha\), we need to maximize the above over \(\alpha\). Maximizing over \(\alpha\), we need to understand the behavior of \[ f(t;a) = e^{-t^2/2} \cosh(a t). \] We have \(f'(t;a) = e^{-t^2/2}(a \sinh(at) - t \cosh(at))\). The critical points are then the solution of \[ \tanh(at) = t/a. \] Zero is always a solution. If \(a \le 1\), zero is the only solution and then it is easy to verify that it is the unique maximum of the function. This means that when \(\|y\| < 1\), the MLE for \(\alpha\) is zero. The following plot illustrates the situation:

    If \(a > 1\), then there are also two nonzero solutions, symmetrically positioned around zero. Let us denote them as \(\pm M(a)\) where \(M(a) > 0\). The maximum occurs at these two points, \(t = \pm M(a)\), and the critical point \(t=0\) corresponds to a minimum in this case. This means that when \(\|y\| > 1\), the MLE for \(\alpha\) is \(M(\|y\|)\) (since \(\alpha \ge 0\) by definition). The following plot illustrates the situation:

    Thus, the MLE for \(\alpha\) is \[\begin{align*} \widehat \alpha = \begin{cases} 0 & \|y\| \le 1, \\ M(\|y\|) & \|y\| > 1. \end{cases} %= g(\norm{y}), \quad g(a) = M(a) 1\{a > 1\}. \end{align*}\] We also note that \(M(a)/a \to 1\) as \(a \to \infty\). (Needs checking.)

  4. Asymptotic behavior of \(\|y\|\). Recall that \(\alpha = \|u\|\) is the parameter of interest and the MLE for it, given above, is a function of \(\|y\|\). So we need to study the behavior of \(\|y\|\) as \(n \to \infty\). Recall that \[ y = \theta u + z = \theta \alpha \omega + z \] where \(z \sim N(0,I_n)\). The short argument is that the norm of \(z\) is of the order of \(\sqrt n\) asymptotiically, while the norm of the first term is \(\|\theta \alpha \omega\| =|\theta| \alpha \|\omega\| = \alpha\). Since \(\alpha\) is constant and \(\sqrt{n} \to \infty\), the norm of \(y\) is dominated by the norm of \(z\) and has no information asymptotically about \(\alpha\). This shows that \(\widehat \alpha\) is inconsistent for \(\alpha\).

  5. A bit more precisely, \[\begin{align*} R_n^2 := \frac{\|y\|^2}n = \frac{\alpha^2}n + \frac{\|z\|^2}n + 2 \alpha \frac{\theta\langle\omega,z\rangle}n. \end{align*}\] Note that \(\alpha\) is constant. The first term is \(o(1)\). By the LLN, we have \(\frac1n \|z\|^2 = 1 + o_p(1)\). Since \(\theta\langle\omega, z\rangle \sim N(0,1)\), we have \(\theta\langle\omega,z\rangle = O_p(1)\), hence the third term is \(o_p(1)\). Putting the pieces together, \[\begin{align*} R_n^2 = 1 + o_p(1). \end{align*}\] That is, \(\|y\| = \sqrt n R_n = \sqrt{n}(1+o_p(1))\). With high probability as \(n \to \infty\), we have \(\|y\| > 1\), hence \(\widehat \alpha = M(\|y\|) \sim M(\sqrt n) \sim \sqrt{n}\), that is, \(\widehat \alpha\) goes to infinity and hence inconsistent for \(\alpha\).
